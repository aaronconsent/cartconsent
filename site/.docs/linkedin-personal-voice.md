# LinkedIn Personal Voice & Cadence — Aaron / Consent Resolve

The personal feed is Aaron-the-founder, not the company. People follow people.
Link-drops underperform here — **retire the worker's weekly resource-share to the
personal feed** and replace it with founder posts + the Monday native-video reel.

## Cadence (personal feed)
| Day | Post |
|---|---|
| **Mon** | Founder post **+** native-video reel cross-post (auto, via scheduler) |
| **Wed** | Founder post |
| **Fri** | Founder post |

~3 founder posts/week + 1 video. Sustainable solo; consistency > volume.

## Voice rules
- **First person, founder building-in-public.** Aaron talking, not the brand.
- **Hook on line one.** Then short lines + whitespace (mobile reads vertically).
- **One idea per post.** End on a thought or a real question.
- Dry, specific, numerically precise. **No hype, no exclamation points, no emoji spray.**
- Heartbeat-v2 aligned: competitors as "the big lead sites" (don't name them), $7 / 98-of-100 OK, never claim "% identified."
- 0–2 hashtags max. No "thought leader" voice — sound like a contractor's smart friend who happens to build software.

## Content pillars (rotate)
1. **Industry truth** — how the lead-marketplace model actually works.
2. **Build-in-public** — what we shipped / learned / got wrong.
3. **Contractor empathy** — name the pain they live.
4. **Contrarian take** — a sharp, defensible opinion.
5. **Founder reflection** — why consent-first, why this, what it costs.

## Starter bank (7 — post as-is or riff)

**1 · Industry truth**
A contractor told me he spent $1,900 on shared leads last month and closed one job.
Then he pulled up his website analytics: 2,300 visitors, nearly all anonymous.
He'd already paid to get those people there — then paid again for "leads" that were partly just his own traffic, resold.
That's the whole reason I'm building Consent Resolve. You earned the visit. You shouldn't have to rent it back.

**2 · Build-in-public**
Shipped a small thing this week I'm weirdly proud of:
when a recovered lead hits a contractor's inbox, it shows the page the person was on when they left.
Not a name scraped off a list — a person who was reading your "emergency AC repair" page at 11pm.
Context turns a "lead" into a conversation. The big lead sites never gave you that.

**3 · Contrarian**
Unpopular opinion: the lead marketplaces aren't broken.
They work exactly as designed — for them. Shared leads, surge pricing, no refunds, your pipeline living in their dashboard.
You're not the customer there. You're the inventory.
The fix isn't a better marketplace. It's owning the traffic you already paid for.

**4 · Contractor empathy**
If you run a home-service business, you've felt this:
Lead notification comes in. You call in 90 seconds. Voicemail.
Call again — "who is this?"
Three other companies got the same text the same second.
That's not a lead. It's a footrace you paid to enter. I'm building the opposite.

**5 · Founder reflection (consent)**
We made an early call that cost us some easy "leads": everything we surface is consent-first.
No scraped numbers. No "how'd you get my info" replies. The person opted in and expects your call.
It's slower. It's also the only version of this I can sleep at night building — and the only one that survives the next privacy law.

**6 · The stat**
The number that started all of this: about 98 of every 100 people who visit a contractor's website leave without identifying themselves.
You paid for every one of those clicks.
We hand a chunk of them back as real, consent-first leads — $7, exclusive, never resold.
Same traffic. Different owner.

**7 · Build-in-public / ask**
Question for the contractors here:
what would make you trust a lead source again after getting burned by the big platforms?
Genuinely asking — we're building Consent Resolve around the answers, and the honest ones sting in a useful way.

## Real founder post bank (from the interview — use these first)
Grounded in Aaron's actual answers. Post as-is or tighten.

**1 · The rigged moment**
I didn't decide the lead-gen model was broken in one dramatic moment.
I decided it reading a contractor's call log — five competitors calling the same homeowner inside one minute.
The platforms weren't even hiding it.
That's not a marketplace. It's a race to the bottom, engineered for platform revenue, not your business.

**2 · What it cost me**
Before this, I ran campaigns wired into shared-lead systems.
We paid to generate the demand — then paid again to compete for it when that same demand got resold to competitors.
The wasted spend stung. Looking desperate, calling people who didn't remember opting in, stung more.

**3 · The $85 receipt**
A contractor told me: "I paid $85 for a hot plumbing lead. Called in 90 seconds. The guy says, 'you're the fourth plumber, I already booked someone.'"
He never got to quote.
That's not a lead. It's a receipt for losing.

**4 · The question that is the whole business**
The first reaction to "98 of 100 visitors leave your site anonymous" is silence.
Then: "almost everyone who comes to my site just disappears?"
Then the one that matters: "I've been paying for leads… while ignoring the 98 who came to me first?"

**5 · The belief the industry gets wrong**
The whole industry runs on one belief: more leads = more jobs.
It's wrong. Ten exclusive, high-intent conversations beat 100 shared leads every time.
Volume is what platforms can sell — not what works for you.

**6 · The line we won't cross**
Our hard rule: we never share a homeowner's intent without clear, direct consent tied to one contractor.
No "implied interest." No bundling. No reselling.
Fewer easy leads — but the homeowner expects your call, and you're not another lead mill.

**7 · "I turned my website on for the first time"**
A contractor recovered conversations from previously anonymous visitors and closed jobs — without buying a single shared lead that month.
He said: "This feels like I turned my own website on for the first time."
That's when I knew we weren't optimizing the old model. We were replacing it.

**8 · What I got wrong**
Early on I assumed the value would be obvious. It wasn't.
Contractors have been burned so many times that "new marketing tool" just triggers skepticism.
So we stopped explaining and started proving — real conversations, real jobs, not dashboards.

**9 · What "100 customers in 2026" actually means**
It's not about the number. It's proof a different model can survive —
that contractors can grow without feeding the shared-lead machine.
If 100 can do it, it's not a tactic. It's a viable alternative. That's the wedge.
